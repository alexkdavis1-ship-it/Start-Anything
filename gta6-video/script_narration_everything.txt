If you play GTA Six the way you played GTA Five, you are going to lose your cash, your car, and your clean record on the very first night. Rockstar rebuilt the police, the money, the cars, even the way you steal. So here is everything you need to know before day one, in twelve rules. And rule four is the one that saves your whole first night.
Rule one. Get the download finished now. Rockstar says the game needs up to two hundred and twelve gigabytes, and there's a reported day-one update on top of that. It unlocks at midnight. On U.S. store pages that's listed as midnight Eastern, which is nine P.M. Pacific. It targets thirty frames per second on every console, including the PS5 Pro. And it's single-player only at launch. No online yet.
The standard edition is eighty dollars. The Ultimate is a hundred. And if you buy before November twentieth, you get the Vintage Vice City pack for free, with a classic car, a garage, and outfits.
Rule two. You don't start in Vice City. You start way down in the Leonida Keys, at Jason's place, with almost nothing. The map is roughly twice the size of GTA Five's, split into six regions. And according to Rockstar's Rob Nelson, the parts you haven't visited stay hidden until you actually go there. A lot of the best stuff has no icon at all. So explore.
Vice City has the beaches and the nightlife. Grassrivers is the swamp, where Rockstar says the gators are not the deadliest thing out there. Port Gellhorn is the forgotten coast, full of cheap motels. Ambrosia is sugar country, run by a refinery and a biker gang. And Mount Kalaga, up north, is hunting, fishing, and people who moved there to be left alone.
Rule three. You're playing two people. Jason Duval and Lucia Caminos. Outside of certain missions, you can switch between them, and whoever you're not controlling keeps living their own day. You can call them over, split up, or ride together. And in a chase, you can swap seats, let your partner drive, and shoot out the back.
The first people you meet set the tone. Brian Heder, an old-school smuggler who lets Jason live rent free. Cal Hampton, Jason's conspiracy-minded best friend. And over in Vice City, Boobie Ike, a local legend with a strip club and a record label.
Rule four. The cops need a reason. No witness and no alarm means no stars. But once they're on you, the game tracks exactly what they know. Your face. Your clothes. Your car. Your weapon. Whether a camera caught you. Each one shows up as an icon next to your stars, and each one has its own fix.
Change your whole outfit, not just the hat. Swap the car, or repaint it. Put the gun away. Wear a mask before the job and take it off after. And keep a spare outfit in your trunk. That one habit will get you out of more trouble than any fast car.
Rule five. Read the stars. Previews describe three states. Hollow stars mean a crime was reported, but they don't know it was you. So walk away calmly. Solid stars mean they can see you. Red stars mean you're out of sight, but still inside the search area. And the stars go up to six again. At six, the entire map is hunting you.
Two more things. Police no longer show up on your minimap, so you have to actually watch the road. And they bring dogs, spike strips, and helicopters with searchlights. Sometimes the smart move is to ditch the car and disappear on foot. Rockstar even says the cops won't search a bus unless they saw you get on it. And if either character gets cuffed, it's busted for both.
Rule six. You can rob almost anything. A corner store. A jewelry store. A small bank. No mission marker needed. You can go loud, kick the door in, and race the clock. Or you can go quiet. Pick the lock, kill the cameras, and empty the register before anyone notices.
Safes pay more, but they take longer. Find the code and you keep everything. Blow it open and you damage part of the take. Clerks and customers can fight back. And previews say you'll want to wipe the camera footage before you leave.
Rule seven, and this is the one that hurts. There are three kinds of money. Money in the bank is safe. Cash in your pocket is not. If you die or get busted, you can lose what you're carrying. And you can't deposit from your phone anymore. You need a real A.T.M. So bank your cash before every big job.
Stolen goods are worth nothing until you sell them to a fence. Different fences buy different things, and they pay below full price. But stay loyal, and they start feeding you leads. And here's why all of this matters. Rob Nelson says the story will ask you for cash at certain points, and missions alone won't cover it. Side hustles are not optional.
Rule eight. Stealing cars takes tools now. Old cars open with a slim jim. Newer ones need a key cloner, and you don't get that early. There's a phone app called Wink that scans a parked car and tells you if it's locked, alarmed, or tracked, and what it's worth. And a tracked car can lead the police straight to you.
Carjacking still works, but it's no longer a guarantee. The driver might hand it over, refuse, or pull a gun. And even if you get away clean, the owner can still call it in. A Pay and Spray will repaint a hot car, and fences can strip a tracker once they trust you.
Rule nine. Register a car as soon as you can. You start with three slots. Your personal car's trunk holds your full weapon loadout and your spare clothes. If it's across town, an app called Scooter Bros delivers it for a fee. And every car now has a fuel gauge and engine health, so glance at both before a getaway. Nelson himself ran out of gas in the middle of a police chase.
Rule ten. Guns are limited. You carry two pistols hidden, and up to two long guns that everybody can see. Walk around with a rifle in your hands and people back away, and the police will warn you. Free aim is reportedly the default now, and recoil is heavier. A new ability called Focus slows everything down and marks weak spots. Red is lethal. Yellow just drops them.
Rule eleven. The game is grading you. Each character has a criminal profile that runs from professional to psycho. Hurt only who the job requires, and you stay professional. Shoot first every time, and you slide the other way. Previews say it changes how people treat you and how the story plays out. And push it far enough, and it may never come back.
Rule twelve. Jason and Lucia's relationship is in your hands. It's measured on four levels, and it grows when they spend time together. Walks on the beach, the gym, kayaking, even robberies. Nelson says it's optional and gives you no gameplay advantage, but it does affect the story. Ignore their texts, and it slips.
Your body changes too. Lift consistently and you build muscle. Live on fast food and you gain weight. Skip sleep and it shows on your face. Coffee boosts stamina. A protein shake boosts health. None of it is forced on you.
And the world pushes back. Rockstar told the New York Times that more than six hundred thousand animations went into the people of Leonida. They remember how you treated them. Storms are local, so it can pour across town while you stand in sunshine. There are more than a hundred and seventy animal species. And there are twenty-three radio stations, plus podcasts you can play on foot.
You can talk to almost anyone. Greet them, taunt them, calm them down, or make it worse. Stand close and you can listen in, and some of those conversations turn into jobs. But stare too long, and they notice. And plenty of regular people are armed. In one demo, a man Nelson had just robbed came after him the second he turned his back.
Getting around is easier than you think. Buses and trains actually run. A ride-share app called Ride Me works like fast travel. And you can drop a waypoint without even opening the map.
Now, what's gone. Previews say there is no full first-person mode on day one. You can't buy houses. Safehouses come from the story, though you can still buy garages. You can't post on Snapmatic, because your characters are on the run. And you can't rock a flipped car back onto its wheels.
So here's your day-one checklist. One. Bank your cash at an A.T.M. before every job. Two. Keep a spare outfit in the trunk. Three. Register a car early. Four. Mask on before a robbery, mask off after. Five. Take your partner along, because jobs together build the relationship.
Rob Nelson's own playthrough reportedly ran about eighty hours. So don't rush it. Play like a criminal with something to lose. Because in this game, you finally have something to lose. Subscribe, and I'll see you in Leonida.
